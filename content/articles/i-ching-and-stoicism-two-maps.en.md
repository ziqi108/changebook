Around the year 170 CE, in a Roman fortress on the Danube, a man named Marcus Aurelius sat down to write to himself. "You have power over your mind — not outside events," he reminded himself in his Meditations. "Realize this, and you will find strength."

More than a millennium earlier, on the other end of the Eurasian landmass, the compilers of the I Ching had reached a strikingly similar insight — though they framed it differently. The Book of Changes speaks less of controlling the mind than of discerning the moment: knowing when the current is with you, when it is against you, and when it is about to turn.

> "You have power over your mind — not outside events. Realize this, and you will find strength." — Marcus Aurelius, Meditations

## Two Maps, One Territory

Two maps, then, drawn from two ends of the ancient world. Neither is complete on its own. Together, they form a more useful picture than either offers alone.

### The Stoic Map

The Stoic map is inward: the world is as it is; your task is to govern your response. Consider a difficult conversation — with a partner, a colleague, a parent. The Stoic asks: What is within my control here?

- My words
- My tone
- My refusal to be dragged into reactivity

The rest — their mood, their history, the outcome — is not mine to manage. This is true, and freeing.

### The I Ching Map

The I Ching map is relational: the world is in motion; your task is to read the motion and place yourself within it well. But the I Ching adds a question the Stoics tended to underplay: What is the time of this moment?

- Is this the hour to speak, or the hour to wait?
- Is the relationship in a season of opening, or a season of withdrawal?
- Which way is the wind blowing before you play the instrument?

The Stoic cleans the instrument; the I Ching asks which way the wind is blowing before you play it.

> "When one is in the right way, one may undertake something. When the right way is not there, one should not." — from the commentary on Hexagram 2, Kūn

## A Daily Practice

A simple practice brings the two together:

1. **Morning (Stoic question):** Before the day begins, sit for two minutes. Ask: What is genuinely within my power today? Name two or three things. Write them down.
2. **Evening (I Ching question):** After the day is done, sit for two minutes. Ask: What time was this day? Was it a season for action, for patience, for repair? Write that down, too.

:::callout Over a month, you will notice something. The Stoic question sharpens your sense of agency. The I Ching question deepens your sense of timing. The first makes you less reactive; the second makes you less rigid. :::

Together, they train something the ancient world called practical wisdom — the capacity to act well, in a particular situation, on a particular day.

Two maps. One territory. Carry both.
